How pressure is measured

Every ball gets a win probability from DLS-style resource curves fitted to the data. Its Pressure Index is how much that probability can swing on the ball. The top 25% of balls in each innings are pressure balls.

Full methodology in brief
  1. Clean and join all six datasets. Wides are not balls faced, byes and leg-byes are not charged to the bowler, run-outs are not bowler wickets, super overs are excluded and rain-revised targets are used.
  2. Resource curves: expected runs left = Z(w) × (1 − e−k(w) × balls left) for each number of wickets in hand, the DLS form, adjusted for each season's IPL and T20I scoring level.
  3. Win probability for every ball: chasing side = chance of scoring the runs needed; side batting first = chance its projected total beats an average chase.
  4. Pressure Index = expected swing in win probability on the ball, ×1.2 in knockouts. Pressure balls are the top 25% within each innings (cutoffs 1.02 in the first innings and 1.52 in the second).
  5. Value per ball = runs and wickets compared with what is expected in the same innings, phase, wickets lost, competition and pace or spin. A wicket costs the runs it takes away from the team at that moment.
  6. Fairness: at least 120 pressure balls; empirical-Bayes (DerSimonian-Laird) shrinkage for small samples; lower weight for games against Associate sides (0.5) and Ireland or Zimbabwe (0.8).
  7. PULSE score = 40% pressure value + 25% win probability added + 20% lift over own normal + 15% consistency, each as a z-score.
  8. Stress tests: 300 bootstrap resamples of matches, five weight settings (at most one name changes) and minimum-ball cutoffs from 60 to 200 (the top 8 batters never change).
